\begin{abstract}
Small-object detectors for drone imagery are usually built by attaching modules to
a YOLO baseline, with gains of one to three points from single runs. We asked which
design decisions still matter once training budget, evaluation settings and seed
are controlled. Using YOLOv11s on VisDrone-DET2019, we
crossed progressive and single-resolution training at a matched epoch budget with
detection heads with and without a stride-4 level, and tested multi-scale edge
information selection (MEIS) with CBAM-style recalibration. Differences were tested
with paired bootstrap tests and analysed by object size.
Single-phase training at 1280\,px beat the progressive schedule by 3.3 points mAP@50
without the stride-4 level and 3.9 points with it ($p<0.002$, and 4.2 points on a
second seed), but the gain came mainly from medium and large objects. The stride-4 level raised AP@50 by 3 to 7
points below 16 input pixels and lowered it by 1 to 2 points above 32, so mAP@50 rose
by only 0.7 points. MEIS with recalibration changed mAP@50 by $+0.6$ and $-0.3$
points on two seeds and improved no size range below 32 pixels; a factorial ablation
found no benefit from multi-scale dilation or adaptive gating over a plain
convolution. A sampling account fits the architectural results---stride sets how
densely small objects are sampled, and modules after downsampling cannot restore what
was lost---but not the schedule effect. Applied to UAVDT without retraining, the
models kept their ordering on cars, though its eleven sequences could not resolve it;
UAVDT has only vehicle classes, so small non-vehicle generalization remains untested.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Single-resolution training beat a four-phase resolution schedule by 3--4 pp
\item Its gain came mostly from medium and large objects, not from small ones
\item A stride-4 level gained 3--7 AP below 16 px and lost 1--2 AP above 32 px
\item Edge selection and recalibration did not improve small objects on two seeds
\item Neither multi-scale dilation nor adaptive gating beat a plain convolution
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================================

Drones photograph scenes from tens to hundreds of metres above the ground, so the
objects that matter for traffic monitoring, crowd counting or search and rescue
often cover only a few dozen pixels~\cite{visdrone2019,uav-survey}. In the
VisDrone-DET2019 training set, 63\% of the annotated instances have a
geometric-mean side shorter than 32 pixels in the original frame. After the
frame is resized for the network, such an object falls on a handful of cells of
a stride-8 feature map, and a detector that sees only a few samples of an object
has little to work with, however its later layers are designed.

Much of the recent work on this problem modifies the network. Attention blocks,
edge enhancement branches, weighted feature fusion and extra prediction heads
have all been attached to YOLO baselines~\cite{tph-yolo,meis-yolo,cf-yolo}, and
the reported improvements are usually modest; MEIS-YOLO, for example, reports
1.4 points of AP@50 over its YOLOv11 baseline~\cite{meis-yolo}. Two things make
gains of that size hard to interpret. If the proposed model and its baseline are
not trained under the same conditions, a gain that comes from a longer schedule
or a larger input can end up credited to the new module. And networks trained
from different random seeds reach different
accuracies~\cite{bouthillier2021variance}; for detectors on VisDrone we measured
seed-to-seed differences of up to 0.7 points, which is comparable to the effects
being claimed.

This paper reports what happened when we removed those confounds. We started from
YOLOv11s~\cite{yolov11} and three modules that target the usual explanations for
poor small-object accuracy: a multi-scale edge information selection (MEIS) block
built from dilated depthwise-separable branches with input-adaptive gating,
following the idea of MEIS-YOLO~\cite{meis-yolo}; a cross-scale weighted fusion
neck in the spirit of CF-YOLO~\cite{cf-yolo} and BiFPN~\cite{efficientdet}; and a
CBAM-style recalibration module~\cite{cbam} placed after fusion. We also studied
two changes that act on sampling rather than on features: the input resolution
used for training, reached either directly or through a four-phase progressive
schedule, and a stride-4 (P2) detection level. Every comparison uses a matched
epoch budget and one evaluation protocol. The module comparison and both schedules
of the four-level detector were repeated with independent seeds, and all differences
were tested with paired resampling over the validation images and broken down by
object size.

The results did not follow the module-centred story we expected. Single-resolution
training at 1280\,px was 3.3 to 4.2 points better than the progressive schedule at
the same epoch budget, with and without a stride-4 level, and it reached the
schedule's final accuracy with one third of its compute. Split by object size,
however, this gain came mostly from medium and large objects. The stride-4 level
behaved as a change in sampling should: it raised accuracy on objects spanning
fewer than two stride-8 cells and lowered it slightly on large objects, which left
overall mAP almost unchanged. MEIS with recalibration produced no significant
change on either of two seeds and no gain for small objects, and a factorial
ablation showed that neither of the two ingredients that define MEIS, multi-scale
dilation and input-adaptive gating, did better than a plain convolution with the
same number of parameters. Section~\ref{sec:sampling} sets out the sampling
argument behind these tests. It accounts for the stride and module results but not
for the schedule effect.

The paper makes three contributions: (i)~a matched-budget comparison of
progressive and single-resolution training for small-object detection, crossed
with the detection stride, with measured compute and paired significance tests;
(ii)~a controlled evaluation of edge selection, weighted fusion and recalibration
modules, including a factorial ablation that separates multi-scale dilation,
adaptive gating and added capacity; and (iii)~a sampling account that gives, for a
given input size and stride, the smallest object that can receive positive training
samples, tested with a size-resolved evaluation that supports it for the detection
stride and the modules and rules it out as the explanation of the schedule effect.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Feature modules for small aerial objects}

Feature pyramids~\cite{fpn,panet} and weighted bidirectional
fusion~\cite{efficientdet} are the standard way to give a detector features at
several scales, and channel or spatial attention~\cite{senet,cbam,ecanet} is
often added to suppress the cluttered backgrounds of aerial scenes. Detectors
designed for drone imagery combine these ideas. TPH-YOLOv5~\cite{tph-yolo} adds
transformer prediction heads, CBAM and an extra prediction head for tiny objects.
MEIS-YOLO~\cite{meis-yolo} inserts an edge information selection block, an
asymptotic feature pyramid and bi-level routing attention into YOLOv11.
CF-YOLO~\cite{cf-yolo} replaces the YOLOv5s neck with cross-fusion blocks for
detection in snow. These papers report their gains without estimates of
run-to-run variation, which makes differences of a point or two difficult to
judge. When a design adds a higher-resolution head together with new blocks, as
TPH-YOLOv5 does, the change in sampling alone may account for part of the gain.

\subsection{Resolution and sampling in aerial detection}

A second line of work treats small objects as a resolution problem. Coarse-to-fine
detectors such as ClusDet~\cite{clusdet}, DMNet~\cite{li2020dmnet} and
UFPMP-Det~\cite{huang2022ufpmp} locate object-dense regions and process them at
higher magnification. QueryDet~\cite{yang2022querydet} runs a cheap coarse pass
and computes high-resolution features only where small objects are likely, and
SAHI~\cite{akyon2022sahi} slices large images into overlapping tiles. These
methods show that magnification helps, but they also change the inference pipeline
and its cost. We look at the two simplest sampling controls available inside a
single-pass detector: the input resolution used in training and the stride of the
finest detection level.

\subsection{Resolution schedules during training}

Curriculum learning~\cite{curriculum-learning} orders training from easy to hard,
and progressive resizing applies a similar idea to image resolution.
EfficientNetV2~\cite{tan2021efficientnetv2} trains image classifiers on small
images first and raises resolution and regularization together, mainly to shorten
training. Touvron et al.~\cite{touvron2019fixres} showed that test-time resolution
interacts with training resolution, so a classifier can benefit from being tested
at a larger size than it was trained on. Both lines of work concern
classification. For dense detection of tiny objects, where a low training
resolution can shrink objects below the size a detection level can represent, we
are not aware of a matched-budget comparison between progressive and
single-resolution training.

\subsection{Variance and significance in detector evaluation}

Networks trained with different seeds, data orders and initializations reach
different accuracies, and Bouthillier et al.~\cite{bouthillier2021variance} showed
that ignoring this variation leads to unreliable conclusions in machine learning
benchmarks. When a metric is computed over a test set, paired resampling
tests~\cite{efron1993bootstrap,bergkirkpatrick2012significance} offer a practical
check, because both models are scored on the same resampled images. Detection
papers rarely report them. We apply them at the image level and report separately
how much results move between seeds.

\section{A Sampling View of Small Object Detection}
\label{sec:sampling}
% ============================================================================

A detector does not see an object at its original size. A frame whose longer side
is $W_0$ pixels is resized so that this side equals the network input size $S$,
which scales an object of side $a$ to $ka$ with $k=S/W_0$. A feature level with
stride $r$ then samples the resized image once every $r$ pixels, so the number of
samples across the object is
\begin{equation}
  n = \frac{k\,a}{r} = \frac{S\,a}{r\,W_0}.
  \label{eq:samples}
\end{equation}
Seen as a sampling problem, $n$ is the sampling rate relative to the object.
With fewer than about two samples per side, a level cannot represent the object's
spatial structure, and the layers that follow inherit the
loss~\cite{shannon1949communication,zhang2019antialiased}.

For anchor-free YOLO detectors the consequence is more direct. YOLOv11 assigns
training targets with the task-aligned assigner of TOOD~\cite{feng2021tood}, which
only considers anchor points whose centres lie inside a ground-truth box. At a
given level an object covers roughly $n_w n_h$ anchor points, where $n_w$ and
$n_h$ count samples along its width and height. When both counts are below one,
the object may contain no anchor point at that level. If that happens at every
level, the object contributes no positive sample and is learned only as
background. Setting $n$ to one or two in Eq.~\eqref{eq:samples} gives the smallest
object side that can be assigned a target, and the smallest that is sampled twice
per side:
\begin{equation}
  a_{\min} = \frac{n\, r\, W_0}{S}.
  \label{eq:amin}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling} illustrates the effect and Table~\ref{tab:amin} evaluates
Eq.~\eqref{eq:amin} for frames 2000 pixels wide, which is about the size of the
largest VisDrone-DET2019 frames. Halving the stride and doubling the input size
have the same effect on $a_{\min}$. Measured directly in network-input pixels, the
geometric-mean side $\sqrt{wh}$ of 31.0\% of the VisDrone validation instances is
smaller than one stride-8 cell at an input size of 640\,px, and that of 5.8\% is
smaller than one stride-4 cell. At 1280\,px, 5.8\% of the instances (the same
objects) are smaller than one stride-8 cell and 0.2\% smaller than one stride-4
cell.

The argument leads to three predictions that the experiments can check.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[P1.] Training at a larger input size, or adding a finer detection level,
  should help most on small objects, because both raise $n$ for the same frames. A
  stride-4 level in particular should help objects that span fewer than about two
  stride-8 cells and matter little for larger ones.
  \item[P2.] A module that operates on stride-$r$ features cannot raise $n$. It can
  reweight what was sampled, but it cannot give training signal to objects that
  received no positive samples, so it should not improve small objects the way a
  finer stride does.
  \item[P3.] A progressive schedule that starts at low resolution spends its early
  epochs in a regime where many small objects cannot be assigned targets
  (Table~\ref{tab:amin}, 640 and 800\,px). Under a fixed epoch budget it should
  therefore do worse than training at the final resolution throughout, and the
  deficit should be largest for small objects.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:res-size} tests all three predictions by object size.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:amin} is a simplification. Mosaic and scale augmentation change $k$
from one training image to the next, context around an object can support
detection, and the assigner's behaviour near box borders depends on implementation
details. We use the argument to organise the experiments, not as a quantitative
model of accuracy.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

We use VisDrone-DET2019~\cite{visdrone2019}, which provides 6,471 training images
and 548 validation images annotated with ten object categories. All results in
this paper are on the validation set; we did not use the test-dev split.
Annotations were converted to the YOLO format, keeping categories 1 to 10 and
discarding ignored regions (category~0) and the \emph{others} class (category~11).
Boxes were clipped to the image, and boxes one pixel wide or high or smaller after
clipping were removed. The converted training set contains 305,974 instances and
the validation set 38,759. The class distribution is strongly skewed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:classes}): cars and pedestrians account for 65.5\% of training
instances, while tricycles, awning-tricycles and buses together make up 4.3\%.

\paragraph{Cross-dataset evaluation}
For the zero-shot test of Section~\ref{sec:res-uavdt} we use UAVDT~\cite{uavdt}, a
second aerial benchmark recorded at $1080\times540$ pixels and annotated with three
vehicle classes: car, truck and bus. Our copy of its detection benchmark holds 11 of
the 50 sequences, four from the official test split and seven from the training
split. Since nothing here is ever trained on UAVDT, all eleven can be used, and they
were not selected by any result. We keep every fifth frame, because the videos run at
30 frames per second and neighbouring frames are nearly identical, which leaves 1,535
images with 12,450 annotated vehicles (11,681 cars, 583 trucks, 186 buses). UAVDT
marks regions of vehicles too small to annotate and excludes them from evaluation: we
drop ground truth inside those regions and, during evaluation, every detection that
lies mostly inside one, about a tenth of all detections. UAVDT has no van class, so
"van" detections are relabelled as car and non-maximum suppression is re-applied to
the merged class. Its objects are larger than VisDrone's: the median geometric-mean
side is 38.5 pixels and 30.7\% of the instances fall below 32 pixels, against 68.6\%
on the VisDrone validation set.

\subsection{Detectors and modules}
\label{sec:models}

All detectors are built on YOLOv11s~\cite{yolov11} and initialized from its
COCO-pretrained weights. The stock model, which we call YOLOv11s (P3--P5), detects
at strides 8, 16 and 32 and serves as the three-level reference in the stride
comparison. The \emph{Baseline-P2} variant extends the top-down path of the neck to the
stride-4 backbone features and starts the bottom-up path from there, which gives
four detection levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Where a modified neck contains C3k2
blocks whose weight shapes match the original head, those weights are copied as
well, so that no variant starts from a randomly initialized block when a
pretrained counterpart exists.

\paragraph{Multi-scale edge information selection (MEIS)}
MEIS is applied to each backbone output that feeds the neck. For an input
$X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, three depthwise-separable branches with
dilation $d\in\{1,2,4\}$ produce edge responses $E_d$, and a channel gate computed
from each response weights it:
\begin{equation}
  g_d = \sigma\!\big(W_2\,\delta(W_1\,\mathrm{GAP}(E_d))\big)\in[0,1]^C, \qquad
  Y = X + \sum_{d} g_d \odot E_d ,
  \label{eq:meis}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{GAP}$ is global average pooling, $W_1$ and $W_2$ are $1{\times}1$
convolutions with reduction ratio 16, $\delta$ is ReLU and $\sigma$ the sigmoid.
The gate is recomputed for every input. Our block tests the dilation-and-gating
interpretation of edge selection; the MEIS module of MEIS-YOLO~\cite{meis-yolo}
instead uses adaptive pooling, high-pass edge enhancement and a dual-domain
selection mechanism, so our results do not transfer to that module directly.

\paragraph{Feature recalibration module (FRM)}
FRM applies the channel and spatial attention of CBAM~\cite{cbam} to each neck
output and blends the result residually,
$Y = X + \alpha\,(X_c - X) + \alpha\,(X_s - X_c)$, where $X_c$ and $X_s$ are the
outputs of the channel and spatial attention steps and $\alpha=0.5$.

\paragraph{Factorial variants}
To separate the ingredients of MEIS, we replaced the dilations $(1,2,4)$ with
$(1,1,1)$, which leaves the parameter count unchanged, and replaced the
input-adaptive gate with an input-independent learnable channel weight
$\sigma(\theta_d)$, $\theta_d\in\mathbb{R}^C$. The weights $\theta_d$ are
initialized to zero, so both gate types start close to 0.5. The variant with
dilations $(1,1,1)$ and a static gate is a plain residual block of
depthwise-separable convolutions with the same capacity as MEIS and no multi-scale
or adaptive component.

\paragraph{Cross-scale weighted fusion}
This neck, evaluated only in the earlier experiments of Appendix~\ref{app:early},
combines resized and channel-projected pyramid features $\tilde F_i$ with
normalized learnable weights,
$F = \sum_i \frac{\mathrm{ReLU}(w_i)}{\sum_j \mathrm{ReLU}(w_j)+\epsilon}\,\tilde F_i$,
followed by a depthwise-separable convolution, similar to the fast normalized
fusion of BiFPN~\cite{efficientdet}.

Table~\ref{tab:complexity} lists the size and computational cost of each variant.
Measured throughput and operation counts do not agree. The stride-4 level costs
74\% more operations and 89\% more time, but MEIS with FRM costs 18\% more
operations and 85\% more time, so its measured cost is 1.6 times what its
operation count implies. Its dilated branches and gating act on the stride-4 map,
where the arithmetic per loaded value is low, and they add many small kernels;
neither shows up in a FLOP count. The two effects compound: the full model runs
3.5 times slower than YOLOv11s at 1280\,px for 2.1 times the operations.

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}

Table~\ref{tab:schedules} lists the two schedules. The \emph{progressive} schedule
trains for 100, 100, 100 and 50 epochs at 640, 800, 1024 and 1280\,px. Each phase
restarts a cosine learning-rate schedule with warm-up, and augmentation strength
decreases while the box and classification loss weights increase from phase to
phase. The \emph{single-phase} schedule trains for up to 350 epochs at 1280\,px
with the phase-1 settings. Both use SGD with momentum 0.937 and weight decay
$5\times10^{-4}$, mixed precision, and the exponential moving average of weights
provided by the Ultralytics trainer. Physical batch sizes were set by GPU memory
and differ between the three- and four-level detectors (Table~\ref{tab:schedules}).
The trainer accumulates gradients to a nominal batch of 64 images, which gave an
effective batch of 60 to 66 images in every phase of every run, so the physical
batch size mainly changed the number of images over which batch-normalization
statistics were computed. Early stopping with a patience of 200 epochs affected
only the single-phase YOLOv11s run, which stopped at epoch 345, 200 epochs after
its best checkpoint.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

All models were evaluated with the Ultralytics validator using letterbox resizing
to input size $S$, a confidence threshold of 0.001, non-maximum suppression at an
IoU of 0.5 and at most 600 detections per image. The validation images contain
70.7 annotated instances on average. For every run we evaluated the checkpoint
with the best validation fitness recorded during training, as the Ultralytics
trainer selects it. We report mAP@50 and mAP@50:95, and precision and recall at
the confidence threshold that maximizes the mean F1 score. AP follows Ultralytics
8.4: the precision envelope is sampled at 101 recall levels, set to zero beyond
the highest recall reached, and integrated with the trapezoidal rule. Some earlier
releases, including Ultralytics 8.3.91, extend the curve to a recall of one, which
for identical detections raised mAP@50 by 0.5 to 0.7 points on our models. The
primary input size is 1280\,px, the final training resolution of both schedules;
we also report 1536\,px.

\paragraph{Accuracy by object size}
To test the sampling account, we split AP@50 by object size. For each validation
image we recorded the detections and, from inside the validator, the ground-truth
box each detection was matched to, so the matching is the validator's own and the
all-sizes result reproduces the reported mAP@50. Object size is the geometric-mean
side $\sqrt{wh}$ of a box. We use it in original-image pixels for the COCO
ranges~\cite{coco} (small below 32, medium 32 to 96, large above 96) and in
network-input pixels at the evaluated size for four ranges, 4--8, 8--16, 16--32 and
at least 32 pixels, which relate directly to stride-4 and stride-8 cells. Within a
range we follow the COCO convention: ground truth outside the range is ignored, as
are detections matched to it and unmatched detections whose own size lies outside
the range. Several classes have only a few boxes in some ranges, so we average AP
over the classes with at least 100 ground-truth boxes in the range. This keeps all
ten classes for small and medium objects, cars and vans for large objects, and
between four and ten classes for the input-pixel ranges.

\subsection{Statistical testing}
\label{sec:stats}

Differences between two models were tested on the 548 validation images with two
paired procedures. Per-image detection statistics were captured from the validator
with batch size one, and recomputing mAP from them reproduces the validator's
reported value. In the \emph{paired bootstrap}~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}, the
images were resampled with replacement 1000 times, both models were scored on each
resample, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the difference form the 95\%
confidence interval. The two-sided $p$-value is twice the smaller fraction of
resampled differences lying on either side of zero; when no resample crosses zero
we report $p<0.002$, the smallest value that 1000 resamples can resolve. Contrasts
that involve more than two models, such as the interaction between schedule and
stride, and all size-resolved differences use the same image resamples for every
model. In the \emph{paired permutation test}, the two models'
statistics were swapped independently for each image with probability 0.5, 1000
times, and $p=(m+1)/1001$, where $m$ counts permutations whose absolute difference
in mAP@50 is at least the observed one. These tests describe uncertainty from the
choice of evaluation images for fixed trained models. Variation between training
seeds is reported separately, since two seeds are too few for a seed-level
interval. On UAVDT the resampling unit is the video sequence rather than the image,
because frames within a sequence are nearly identical and resampling them would
understate the uncertainty. Because ten classes are tested at once in the per-class comparisons, we
treat a class difference as significant only when it survives a Bonferroni
correction ($p<0.005$).

\subsection{Detection stride}
\label{sec:res-stride}

Table~\ref{tab:stride} crosses the two schedules with the two detection heads, all
trained with seed 0. Under single-phase training the stride-4 level added 0.73
points mAP@50 at 1280\,px (95\% CI $[0.14, 1.26]$, $p=0.012$; permutation
$p=0.019$) and 0.46 points mAP@50:95 ($[0.05, 0.83]$, $p=0.028$). At 1536\,px the
difference shrank to 0.37 points ($[-0.14, 0.88]$, $p=0.16$), and under the
progressive schedule it was $-0.43$ points ($[-1.15, 0.26]$, $p=0.27$). The gain
from the stride-4 level was 0.8 points larger under single-phase training than
under the progressive schedule ($[-0.08, 1.74]$, $p=0.07$ at 1536\,px). With one
seed per configuration and seed-to-seed differences of up to 0.7 points for a
single model, we do not treat this interaction as established. The three-level
runs also used larger physical batches than the four-level runs under both
schedules (Section~\ref{sec:training}).

At the class level, the stride-4 level helped the classes with the smallest boxes.
With single-phase training at 1280\,px, AP@50 rose by 3.9 points for pedestrians
($[3.1, 4.7]$), 4.2 points for people ($[3.1, 5.4]$), 2.2 points for motorcycles
($[1.3, 3.1]$) and 0.9 points for cars ($[0.6, 1.4]$), all $p<0.002$. Tricycles
and awning-tricycles lost 2.1 and 1.9 points ($p=0.016$ and $0.028$), which does
not survive the correction for ten classes, and the remaining classes did not
change significantly. Section~\ref{sec:res-size} shows that these class
differences follow object size.

The stride-4 level is expensive. It adds 0.49M parameters and raises GFLOPs by
74\% at every input size (Table~\ref{tab:complexity}), because it operates on a
feature map with four times as many positions as the stride-8 level. A larger
input is the cheaper way to reach the same accuracy: YOLOv11s evaluated at 1536\,px
scored 58.47\% mAP@50 with 124 GFLOPs, more than Baseline-P2 at 1280\,px with 150
GFLOPs (57.26\%).

\subsection{Feature modules}
\label{sec:res-modules}

progressive schedule. The change in mAP@50 was $+0.56$ points with seed 0 and

\subsubsection{Factorial ablation of MEIS}
\label{sec:res-factorial}

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} and Fig.~\ref{fig:factorial} separate the two ingredients
of MEIS under the shortened schedule. All four MEIS variants include FRM, and the
reference model has neither module. The model without modules was the most
accurate, and removing either ingredient of MEIS made results slightly better
rather than worse. Averaged over paired same-seed differences at 1280\,px,
multi-scale dilation changed mAP@50 by $-0.20$ points and input-adaptive gating by
$-0.24$ points, each negative in all four pairings, with a negligible interaction
($-0.06$). Full MEIS scored 0.44 points below the plain convolution of equal size.
At 1536\,px the effects pointed the same way ($-0.16$ and $-0.18$ points), in three
of four pairings each. With two seeds, differences of 0.2 points cannot be resolved
statistically; what the ablation does show is that neither ingredient helped.

The module variants trailed the no-module model by 0.6 to 1.0 points in this
75-epoch schedule, whereas under the full schedule the gap was not significant
(Table~\ref{tab:modules}). A plausible reading is that the added blocks slow
optimization early on and, given enough epochs, settle close to an identity mapping
through their residual connections, but we have not tested this directly.

\subsection{Seed variation and image sampling}
\label{sec:res-variance}

The two sources of uncertainty were of different size. For the MEIS and FRM
comparison, the paired difference moved from $+0.58$ to $-0.34$ points at
1536\,px between seeds, a standard deviation of 0.65 points, while the image-level
bootstrap standard error was 0.35 points. Individual models also moved: Baseline-P2
scored 53.32\% and 53.60\% at 1280\,px with the two seeds, and the MEIS model
53.88\% and 53.24\%. The single-phase Baseline-P2 pair moved by a comparable
amount, 57.26\% and 57.76\%, which is why the schedule effect survives the seed
change and the module effect does not.
Two seeds give only a rough estimate of the seed variance, but
the comparison is enough to show that a single seed would have been misleading
here. Seed 0 alone suggests that the modules add about half a point; seed 42
suggests that they remove a third of one. For effects below one point we would
recommend at least three seeds together with paired tests over the evaluation
images.

\subsection{Accuracy by object size}
\label{sec:res-size}

Table~\ref{tab:size} gives AP@50 in the COCO size ranges, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:size} shows the three comparisons by object size in network-input
pixels, the scale on which the predictions of Section~\ref{sec:sampling} are made.

\paragraph{Detection stride (P1)}
The stride-4 level changed accuracy where the sampling argument says it should
(Fig.~\ref{fig:size}a). For objects of 4 to 8 input pixels it added 4.0 points
AP@50 under the progressive schedule ($[1.7, 6.6]$) and 6.7 points under
single-phase training ($[4.4, 9.0]$), and for objects of 8 to 16 pixels 2.8 and
4.9 points ($[1.6, 3.9]$ and $[3.7, 6.1]$). For objects of 16 to 32 pixels it
changed nothing (0.4 and 0.1 points), and for objects of at least 32 pixels it
lowered AP@50 by 2.1 and 1.1 points ($[-3.0, -1.0]$ and $[-1.9, -0.3]$). The gain
thus disappears once objects span about two stride-8 cells and turns into a loss
beyond four, and the losses on larger objects explain why mAP@50 barely moved. Cars, which have at least 655
boxes in every range, show the same pattern on their own: 2.9 to 4.0 points below
16 pixels and less than one point above.

\paragraph{Feature modules (P2)}
MEIS with FRM changed AP@50 by between $-1.2$ and $+0.3$ points in the three
ranges below 32 pixels on the two seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:size}c), and only one of
these six intervals excluded zero, the $-1.2$ points for 8 to 16 pixels with seed
42. Averaged over both seeds, COCO small objects lost 0.8 points ($[-1.5, -0.1]$).
The only significant gains, 1.7 points for objects of at least 32 pixels and 1.4
points for COCO medium objects with seed 0, did not recur with seed 42 (0.1 and
$-0.1$ points). Under the same schedule and on the
same objects, the stride-4 level gained 2.8 to 4.0 points below 16 pixels. This is
what P2 predicts: reweighting features after downsampling did not help the objects
that a finer stride did help.

\paragraph{Training schedule (P3)}
Single-phase training did not concentrate its gain on small objects
(Fig.~\ref{fig:size}b). For YOLOv11s it changed AP@50 by $-0.6$ and $+0.5$ points
for objects below 16 input pixels, neither significant, but added 2.7 points for
16 to 32 pixels and 5.5 points for objects of at least 32 pixels ($[4.6, 6.7]$).
In the COCO ranges the gains were 1.6 points for small, 5.2 for medium and 6.1 for
large objects. With the stride-4 level, single-phase training also helped objects
of 8 to 16 pixels, by 2.7 points, and this exceeded the corresponding gain for
YOLOv11s by 2.1 points ($[0.8, 3.5]$). The largest gain was again for objects of
at least 32 pixels (6.5 points, $[5.6, 7.6]$). P3 predicted the opposite ordering.

A mismatch between training and test scale could produce a deficit on larger
objects: the progressive models saw objects at smaller pixel sizes for 300 of their
350 epochs. If that were the cause, evaluating them at 1024\,px would raise their AP
on medium and large objects. It did not. Medium objects changed by $-0.9$ points
($[-1.8, -0.1]$) for YOLOv11s and $+0.1$ points ($[-0.8, 0.9]$) for Baseline-P2,
and large objects by less than 0.5 points. At 1024\,px, single-phase training still
led on medium objects by 4.5 and 4.1 points (both $p<0.002$). The single-phase
models were, however, more tied to their training size: evaluated at 1024 instead
of 1280\,px, they lost 1.0 points more than the progressive models on small objects
($[0.3, 1.7]$) and 1.3 points more on medium objects ($[0.7, 2.1]$).

\subsection{Cross-dataset evaluation on UAVDT}
\label{sec:res-uavdt}

Table~\ref{tab:uavdt} applies the VisDrone-trained models to UAVDT without further
training. On cars, the only class annotated in every sequence, the ordering of
Sections~\ref{sec:res-schedule} and~\ref{sec:res-stride} reappears. Single-phase
training added 2.24 points AP@50 without the stride-4 level and 2.47 points with it,
the stride-4 level added 1.82 points under the progressive schedule and 2.05 points
under single-phase training, and MEIS with FRM subtracted 0.81 points. None of these
differences survives resampling of the eleven sequences: the intervals are
$[-0.2, 5.5]$, $[-0.1, 5.7]$, $[-0.5, 4.2]$, $[-0.1, 4.3]$ and $[-2.9, 1.7]$ points.
Resampling frames instead would have given intervals several times tighter, and a
false impression of precision, because consecutive frames of a video are nearly the
same image.

Trucks and buses cannot carry a conclusion here. Trucks appear in six of the eleven
sequences with 62\% of them in one, and buses in four sequences with 62\% in one, so
their AP moves by tens of points between resamples and the two classes rank the
models in opposite orders. That is also why the three-class mean, which weights them
equally with cars, reverses the car ordering.

This evaluation therefore supports a narrow claim: the accuracy ordering on the
dominant class survives a change of dataset, and the modules do not help on a second
benchmark either. It says nothing about small objects outside VisDrone, since UAVDT
annotates only vehicles and its objects are larger (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\subsection{Relation to published results}
\label{sec:res-published}

Published VisDrone results are difficult to compare with each other because
evaluation split, input size, pretraining and inference procedure differ between
papers. Table~\ref{tab:published} shows the problem with the closest related
method. MEIS-YOLO~\cite{meis-yolo} reports 36.0\% AP@50 on the VisDrone-DET2019
test set, for a network trained from scratch at 640\,px, against 34.6\% for its
YOLOv11m baseline. Our models were evaluated on the validation set, start from
COCO weights and use 1280\,px inputs, so the numbers are not on a common scale and
the table should not be read as a ranking. The evaluation code matters as well:
Ultralytics releases that extend the precision--recall curve to a recall of one
give mAP@50 values 0.5 to 0.7 points higher than the definition we use for the
same detections (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). Region-based detectors that process
magnified crops~\cite{clusdet,li2020dmnet,huang2022ufpmp} use several network
passes per image and belong to a different accuracy--cost regime.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{What the sampling account explains}

The size breakdown supports two of the three predictions of
Section~\ref{sec:sampling}. The stride-4 level raised accuracy for the objects the
argument singles out, those spanning fewer than two stride-8 cells, and did not
help larger ones. MEIS and FRM, which act on features that have already
been sampled at stride $r$, did not improve those same objects on either seed,
although a finer stride raised their AP by up to 7 points. Both modules are
residual, so they can stay close to an identity mapping when reweighting does not
help, and the factorial ablation found no benefit from their multi-scale or
adaptive components. Two caveats apply to the module results. Our MEIS block is
not the MEIS module of MEIS-YOLO, and the module experiments used the progressive
schedule, so whether the modules behave differently when trained at 1280\,px
throughout remains untested.

The stride-4 level also lowered AP for objects of at least 32 pixels. We did not
investigate the cause. One possibility is that the extra level changes how the
task-aligned assigner distributes positive samples across levels, which could
leave the coarser levels with fewer positives for large objects.

\subsection{Why did the progressive schedule do worse?}

The schedule effect is the result the sampling account does not explain. If the
low-resolution phases had mainly deprived small objects of training targets, the
deficit would be largest for small objects. It was largest for medium and large
objects instead, and for YOLOv11s it was absent below 16 input pixels. For
Baseline-P2 this is less surprising in hindsight, because even at 640\,px only
5.8\% of the validation objects are smaller than one stride-4 cell. For YOLOv11s it
is harder to reconcile with the account, since 31.0\% of the objects fall below one
stride-8 cell at 640\,px. A mismatch between training and test scale does not
explain the deficit either, since the progressive models did not improve on larger
objects when evaluated at 1024\,px.

The remaining differences between the schedules concern optimization and
regularization. The progressive schedule restarts the learning rate with a warm-up
at every phase boundary, spends its only 50 epochs at 1280\,px with an initial
learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$, and changes augmentation and loss weights
between phases. We did not separate these factors; training a single-resolution schedule
with phase-wise restarts, and a progressive schedule without them, would. The one
part of the schedule effect that fits the sampling account is that Baseline-P2
gained more than YOLOv11s from single-phase training on objects of 8 to 16 pixels,
as expected if the stride-4 level needs high-resolution training to receive
targets for small objects. The checkpoint results also rule out faster convergence
as an argument for the progressive schedule, since single-phase training matched
its final accuracy after 61 epochs.

\subsection{Practical recommendations}

Five recommendations follow from these experiments. First, train at the input size
that will be used at deployment: the single-phase models were the most accurate at
their training size but lost more than the progressive models when evaluated at a
smaller one. Second, before adding modules, compare the object size distribution in
network-input pixels with the finest detection stride. A stride-4 level pays off
when many objects span fewer than two stride-8 cells, but it costs accuracy on
large objects, 74\% more computation and 89\% more time, and a larger input size
may reach the same accuracy at lower cost. Third, report accuracy by object size,
because a small change in mAP can hide opposite effects on small and large
objects. Fourth, compare designs with at least two or three seeds, test paired
differences over the evaluation images, and report training compute alongside
accuracy. Fifth, measure speed where the GPU is the limit, and report the
operation count separately rather than in its place. With one image per batch,
two of our three models took the same time at 1280 and at 1536\,px, because the
host could not submit kernels faster than that; read at face value, those numbers
put the cost of the stride-4 level at 18\% when it is 89\%. A larger batch, or any
check that the time grows with the pixel count, separates the model from the
machine that runs it.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study has several limitations. The module comparison was repeated with two
seeds, and so were both Baseline-P2 schedules, but the two YOLOv11s cells of the
schedule-by-stride comparison rest on one seed each, so the interaction between
schedule and stride is not established. The three- and four-level detectors were
trained with different physical batch sizes, which changes batch-normalization
statistics. Checkpoints were selected on the validation images used for reporting.
This makes absolute accuracy slightly optimistic for every model, and selection
over up to 350 single-phase epochs may favour those runs somewhat more than
selection within the 50-epoch final progressive phase. Size-resolved AP averages
only classes with at least 100 boxes in a range, which leaves two classes for large
objects. The cross-dataset evaluation covers the 11 UAVDT detection sequences
available to us, its truck and bus results rest largely on one sequence each, and
UAVDT annotates only vehicles, which are larger than VisDrone's objects; whether any
of this transfers to small non-vehicle objects outside VisDrone is untested. We studied one
detector family at one size, so larger models and transformer-based detectors may
behave differently; we chose YOLOv11s because it is widely used, has a maintained
implementation with pretrained weights, and is a realistic size for drone
platforms. The schedule comparison changes learning-rate restarts, augmentation
and loss weights together with resolution. The module experiments used the
progressive schedule, and the factorial ablation a shortened one. We did not
evaluate robustness to motion blur, noise or illumination changes. Finally, the
sampling argument is a heuristic, and FPS figures depend on the hardware.

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

For YOLOv11s on VisDrone-DET2019, controlled comparisons separated three kinds of
design decision. The finest detection stride changed small-object accuracy in the
way a sampling argument predicts: a stride-4 level added 3 to 7 points AP@50 for
objects spanning fewer than two stride-8 cells and cost 1 to 2 points on objects
larger than 32 pixels, which left mAP almost unchanged. Edge selection with
adaptive gating and CBAM-style recalibration, which act after downsampling,
improved no size range below 32 pixels on either of two seeds, and neither
multi-scale dilation nor adaptive gating did better than a plain convolution of
equal size, while cutting throughput almost in half, from 97 to 53 images per
second at 1280\,px.
Single-resolution training at 1280\,px outperformed a four-phase
progressive schedule by 3.3 to 4.2 points mAP@50 at the same epoch budget, with and
without the stride-4 level and on both seeds of the four-level detector, and
matched its accuracy with a third of the compute.
Its gain came mainly from medium and large objects and did not vanish at a smaller
test size, so it is better explained by how the two schedules train the network
than by how densely they sample small objects. Applied to UAVDT without retraining,
the ranking held for cars, the only class annotated throughout, although eleven
sequences could not resolve the differences statistically. These results do not show that feature modules
or resolution schedules cannot help small-object detection. They show that such
changes should be compared with baselines trained at the same resolution and
budget, across seeds, with paired tests and a breakdown by object size, before a
gain is attributed to the mechanism it was designed for.
